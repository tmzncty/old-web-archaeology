## 1. Claim list before story

This run tests six narrow claims.

1. By roughly 1998–1999, Chinese gay-oriented websites were functioning as more than publications: personal ads, chat, BBS, health information and local activity coordination were ordinary community functions.
2. These sites could change how users found peers and life scripts without requiring equivalent visibility to family, workplace or school.
3. The early ecosystem was fragile: many sites were individually operated and short-lived, so surviving sites may badly overrepresent the historical field.
4. Women/lesbian users were not represented by the same site supply as gay men; offline hotlines and gatherings remained important.
5. The current GZTZ host exposes records carrying dates from 2000–2003 and later, but those records are **not yet verified historical HTML captures**.
6. A contemporaneous forum quotation embedded in a 2006 news report proves that the report quoted a post; it does **not** automatically prove that the original forum artifact survives.

# 2. Evidence object A: Jiang Hui 2005 operator/conference paper

## Source

Hui Jiang, **“ICCGL: cultural communication via the internet and GLBT community building in China”**, paper presented at *Sexualities, Genders and Rights in Asia*, 2005.

ANU repository landing page:

<https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/items/9aab3336-8840-4a0a-95c8-d7c0df40c72d>

### Evidence grade

**B, high confidence for “an operator reported this in 2005”; medium confidence for national totals.**

It is contemporary to the ecosystem and written from six years of GayChinese.net / ICCGL experience, but it is not an independent census of every Chinese site and it is not an original page artifact.

## Claims supported

The paper reports that:

- early mainland gay websites appeared around 1998;
- sites commonly used personal ads, chatrooms and BBS alongside news, literature, health and advice;
- local sites spread beyond the largest cities and sometimes coordinated offline sports, outings, performances and HIV-prevention activity;
- the author estimated roughly **300–400** GLBT-serving sites in mainland China in 2005;
- most gay sites that had existed had lifecycles of only about **one or two years**, with only about ten lasting six or seven years;
- some people in remote or smaller cities could depend heavily on gay websites as a link to a wider gay population;
- the author estimated fewer than twenty sites served lesbians exclusively among roughly 300 operating gay sites;
- GayChinese.net was established in 1999 by a gay couple in Xiamen and published material about their relationship and ordinary life.

### What this source does **not** prove

- exact national site counts;
- the share of gay/lesbian people with Internet access;
- site popularity distributions;
- 1998/1999 HTML structure;
- historical charset / HTTP headers / server software;
- that every site had all listed features;
- that online community displaced bars, parks, hotlines or print communication.

# 3. Evidence object B: later reconstruction of GZTZ origins

A 2008 investigative history reports that in August 1998 a 24-year-old English graduate using the name “诺哲” established “广州同志” (`www.gztz.org`) after already using 9.6k dial-up for more than a year. The report describes the site as reducing reliance on specific physical meeting points and expanding cross-regional contact.

Sources:

- 凤凰/南方新闻网, 2008: <https://news.ifeng.com/society/5/200809/0919_2579_793906_2.shtml>
- broader historical installment including 1997 BP-pager hotline: <https://news.ifeng.com/society/5/200809/0919_2579_793906_1.shtml>

### Evidence grade

**C / B-C.**

This is a later historical reconstruction with participant interviews. It is useful for recovering remembered chronology and practices, but not sufficient to reconstruct the 1998 website.

### Narrow safe claim

> By 2008, participants and journalists were consistently remembering GZTZ as a late-1990s Guangzhou gay Web community and tying its usefulness to dial-up-era cross-regional peer contact.

Do **not** upgrade this to:

> “This is what the August 1998 page looked like.”

# 5. Why the current GZTZ corpus is nevertheless archaeologically valuable

Even without a verified 2001 capture, the live corpus preserves clues that can guide future artifact work:

- old `本站发表` timestamps;
- old `最后编辑` timestamps;
- page ID patterns such as `doc_431399.htm`;
- category/index relations;
- author/source labels;
- later comments attached to older records;
- user-submitted “self-help information platform” notices;
- the existence of historical record layers spanning multiple years.

These are **locator and data-provenance clues**, not browser reconstruction evidence.

A future M1 case could compare:

```text
current database record
+
verified Wayback capture of same URL or same record
+
verified second historical timepoint
```

and ask whether text, timestamps, URL IDs, category labels and comment systems changed across migrations.

# 6. Evidence object D: 2005–2006 contemporaneous community reports

## Women / lesbian community infrastructure

A 2005 *China Newsweek* report describes Beijing lesbian community practices including fixed-time women-only bar gatherings and the volunteer group 同语, which operated two lesbian hotlines on Sunday afternoons and received more than thirty calls per month according to the report.

Sources (same evidence family):

- <https://news.sina.com.cn/o/2005-09-02/13566847325s.shtml>
- <https://news.sina.com.cn/c/2005-08-25/12187592374.shtml>

Evidence: **B, contemporaneous report.**

This is important because the early Chinese queer Web must not be reconstructed from male-gay sites alone. Jiang 2005 also reports a very small number of lesbian-exclusive sites relative to the overall gay-site estimate.

### Archaeological implication

A Web-only sample can systematically undercount ordinary life infrastructures that used:

- hotlines;
- bars / scheduled meetups;
- printed materials;
- volunteer organizations;
- closed or semi-closed mailing / chat networks;
- websites that were smaller or less durable than major male-gay sites.

This is both an adoption bias and an archive bias.

# 8. New life-pathway finding: peer discoverability before household legibility

The main cross-repository contribution is not a new abstract identity-state taxonomy. Existing notes already cover pseudonymity, real-name linkage and context collapse.

The new pathway is narrower:

```text
person does not follow default heterosexual family script
→ needs evidence that peers exist
→ needs a low-cost / selectively visible entry point
→ peer network becomes searchable
→ advice / partner / ordinary-life examples become observable
→ some ties move offline
→ family/workplace visibility can remain separate
```

For old-Web archaeology, this matters because page types such as:

- personals;
- chatrooms;
- BBS;
- Q&A;
- local activity pages;
- health information;
- venue/self-help listings

were not merely “content genres.” They could be **life-course infrastructure**.

That is a legitimate reason to prioritize their recovery even when the home page is easier to archive.

# 9. Survivor-site bias

Jiang 2005’s report that most sites survived only one or two years is highly consequential for archive sampling.

Define:

**survivor-site bias**

> The sites most visible to researchers decades later are disproportionately likely to be the ones with longer operation, stronger technical capacity, better migration, stronger organizations or durable domains; therefore surviving sites may not represent the modal historical site.

Potential consequences:

- major sites like GZTZ / GayChinese / Aibai can dominate retrospective histories;
- one-person local sites may vanish almost completely;
- personals/chat content can disappear faster than editorial articles;
- lesbian/small-community sites may be doubly underrepresented if fewer existed and fewer survived;
- current search engines favor still-resolving domains and rehosted editorial text.

### Do not make the reverse mistake

Short site lifetime does **not** prove low social value.

A two-year site can still matter intensely to a user whose school, first relationship, coming-out decision or migration occurred during those two years.

# 10. A privacy asymmetry specific to this corpus

Current live GZTZ pages can expose old usernames, comments and in some cases historical contact information.

Repository policy for this corpus:

- do not copy old QQ numbers, phone numbers, emails or personal-ad contact details into research notes;
- do not reproduce sensitive sexual/health content unless it is essential to a structural claim;
- prefer URL patterns, category structure and aggregate behavior;
- when a page is useful only because it reveals ordinary user activity, describe the activity without re-identifying the user;
- `publicly indexable in 2026 != ethically necessary to republish`.

This follows `docs/METHOD.md` section 9 and is especially important where old community pages outlive the user’s reasonable expectation of continued discoverability.

# 13. Overseas comparison: soc.motss and queer BBSs before the Web

## Primary Usenet artifact

A 1991 `soc.motss` FAQ preserved on Google Groups states:

- about 40,000 worldwide readers in 1991;
- more than 100 articles per day;
- the group was created in October 1983 as `net.motss`;
- the FAQ explicitly says a more obvious `soc.gay` name could lead some administrators not to carry the group;
- Steve Dyer’s 1983 announcement described the forum as a place to discuss health, parenting, relationships and job security.

Source:

<https://groups.google.com/g/soc.motss/c/hvqaRx1NEcs/m/aPDDASArBgUJ>

Evidence: **A, contemporaneous network document.**

## Secondary BBS history

Humanities Washington’s history of early queer networking documents GLIB (Gay and Lesbian Information Bureau), founded in 1986, and describes online community feeding into offline friendships and gatherings.

Source:

<https://www.humanities.org/spark/love-acceptance-and-screeching-modems/>

Evidence: **C/B, later public-history synthesis drawing on archival and participant materials.**

### Comparative conclusion

The Chinese case is not unique in using digital networks to make a dispersed sexual minority mutually discoverable.

What varies is the infrastructure stack:

```text
US 1980s:
Usenet / dial-up BBS + university/computer access + local organizations

China late 1990s:
physical meeting points + pager hotline + landline + print/newsletter
+ rapidly growing dial-up Web / BBS / chat
```

Both cases also show **selective legibility**: the community needs to be discoverable to intended peers without necessarily being equally legible to every administrator, employer, family member or stranger.

# 16. What this changes in old-Web archaeology

The important addition is not that “gay websites existed.”

It is that an early Web page can be part of a person’s **life-course executability**:

> a personals form can determine who becomes reachable; a BBS can make an alternative household future visible; a local page can convert a geographically scattered minority into a meetup; a health Q&A can connect an otherwise private life to professional advice.

Therefore an archaeological case should not stop at the homepage screenshot.

For this domain, the historically decisive artifacts may be exactly the ones archives preserve worst:

- forms;
- user posts;
- chat systems;
- personals;
- member-only areas;
- comments;
- local activity notices;
- short-lived sites.

And that creates the central warning of this probe:

> **the more durable and editorial a page is, the easier it may be to study — but the less it may represent the interactive infrastructure that actually changed ordinary people’s lives.**
