# SHIFT WORK JOB LISTINGS, ROSTERS, AND ACTUAL-CLOCK STATE GAPS (2005–2015)

Status: methodology / research note  
Scope: Chinese Web evidence, roughly 2005–2015; not a general labour-law history.  
Purpose: model what a historical Web page can and cannot prove about a worker's real schedule.

## 1. Why shift work is an old-Web archaeology problem

A historical recruitment page or news article can preserve words such as:

- 白班 / 夜班；
- 两班倒 / 三班倒；
- 早班 / 中班 / 晚班；
- 上四休二；
- 轮班 / 倒班；
- 不定时；
- 综合计算工时；
- 加班；
- 值班 / 待命。

These strings look concrete, but they often describe only a **schedule label**, not the full lived schedule.

The main archaeological rule is:

> **advertised schedule ≠ assigned roster ≠ actual clock time ≠ paid time.**

A preserved job ad can show what an employer or intermediary advertised. It normally cannot show which shift a named worker later received, whether the roster changed, whether overtime occurred, whether a private swap happened, or whether all worked hours were recognized in payroll.

This note adds a state model so that old Chinese Web evidence does not silently turn into a fake time diary.

---

## 2. Research units must remain separate

For shift-work evidence distinguish at least:

- **job listing** — public recruitment text;
- **employer / intermediary** — who published or supplied the text;
- **position** — the role being advertised;
- **schedule label** — e.g. 两班倒;
- **schedule rule** — formal rotation/rest arrangement if stated;
- **team / crew** — one group inside the rotation;
- **roster** — dated assignment of a person/team to a shift;
- **clock record** — actual entry/exit event;
- **overtime record** — actual or recognized extra time;
- **payroll state** — whether time affected pay;
- **forum/news testimony** — someone's account of the schedule;
- **archive capture** — preserved representation of one Web resource;
- **claim** — the narrow conclusion supported by the evidence.

Do not collapse these into “the worker worked X hours.”

## 3. Minimum state machine

A useful schedule path is:

```text
job/role exists
→ schedule described or omitted
→ applicant sees information
→ interview/onboarding explains schedule
→ contract / notice / employee handbook states a rule
→ employee assigned to team
→ dated roster issued
→ roster possibly revised
→ worker actually clocks in
→ worker actually clocks out
→ breaks/overtime occur
→ time is recognized or disputed
→ payroll is calculated
→ next roster is issued
```

The public Web often preserves only the first three or four states.

For old-Web archaeology this creates an unusually large **private-state tail**: the more historically important the question becomes for one person's life, the less likely it is to be publicly archived.

## 4. Core gaps

### 4.1 advertised-schedule gap

A listing says “两班倒”. This proves only that the publisher represented the role that way at publication time.

Unknown unless separately supported:

- actual starting/ending clock time;
- rotation period;
- number of teams;
- rest cycle;
- whether overtime was routine;
- whether the schedule changed after recruitment.

### 4.2 team-count gap

Two workplaces can both say “two shifts” while using different crew counts. Two crews covering two shifts and three crews rotating through two daily shifts can produce radically different rest days.

Thus:

> `daily shift count ≠ crew count ≠ worker rest topology`.

### 4.3 rotation-period gap

“白夜班轮换” does not tell us whether rotation happens daily, weekly, every two weeks, monthly, or irregularly.

This matters because circadian and household disruption depend on the sequence, not only the labels.

### 4.4 roster-assignment gap

A company may operate both day and night shifts; that does not establish which one a particular worker received.

### 4.5 schedule-change gap

A roster can be changed after the page was published or after a worker took a screenshot. Historical Web capture freezes the page, not the workplace calendar.

### 4.6 clock-in/out gap

Even a genuine roster is a plan. It does not prove the worker arrived, left on time, or remained after the nominal shift.

### 4.7 overtime-recognition gap

Actual extra work, formally approved overtime, payroll-recognized overtime and paid overtime are different states.

### 4.8 shift-swap gap

Workers can privately exchange shifts. These swaps may exist only in phone calls, SMS, QQ/IM or internal systems and never enter a public page.

### 4.9 rest-day gap

A schedule label often omits monthly rest-day count and the location of rest days in the rotation.

### 4.10 night-infrastructure gap

A listing rarely proves whether the worker had:

- night bus / factory shuttle;
- dormitory;
- canteen at shift end;
- safe route home;
- daytime sleeping conditions;
- childcare compatible with the shift.

### 4.11 family-overlap gap

No ordinary public job ad can tell us whether a worker's free hours overlap with spouse, children, parents or friends.

### 4.12 recovery-time gap

A 08:00 clock-out does not tell us whether the person slept at 09:00, went to an Internet café until 11:00, did housework, queued at a hospital, or looked after a child.

### 4.13 job-listing-liveness gap

A recruitment page can survive in an archive long after the vacancy closed or its shift system changed.

`page accessible at capture time ≠ position still open at capture time`.

## 5. A 2009 case: student internship shows why Web-visible schedule text needs institutional context

A 2009 *Yangcheng Evening News* report, preserved on Sina Education, describes 20-year-old vocational student 薛珍珍（化名）doing an internship in a Huizhou electronics factory.

The article records:

- night shift roughly 19:00–07:00;
- busy periods extending after 08:00;
- 12-hour working days;
- early/night shift rotation every two weeks;
- a training/internship certificate linked to normal graduation;
- her concern that leaving the factory might affect graduation.

Current live locator for the dated 2009 article:
- https://edu.sina.com.cn/j/2009-07-10/1438174300.shtml

Evidence role:
- **B** — contemporaneous newspaper reportage rehosted/preserved on a major portal, including contemporaneous first-person testimony.

It supports a narrow historical claim that this student and the reported cohort were described as living under a two-week rotating early/night shift system.

It does **not** by itself prove:

- the factory's complete official roster;
- every student's actual daily clock records;
- whether all extra minutes were recognized as overtime;
- the prevalence of this arrangement among all vocational internships.

The case is especially useful because the schedule is tied to another institutional state — graduation eligibility. A schedule page cannot be interpreted only as work-time evidence if leaving the shift may also affect an education credential.

## 6. A 2010 case: public news can preserve actual-clock testimony, but still not the payroll backend

A 2010 *Beijing Evening News* report on a multi-university Foxconn investigation says the project used 1,736 questionnaires across 12 factory areas and 14 participant-observer investigators.

One investigator was assigned to a 20:00–08:00 night shift. The same report states that the survey found:

- 75% reporting an average of four rest days per month;
- 73.3% reporting average workdays of 10 hours or more;
- reported average monthly overtime of 83.2 hours.

The article also describes some workers visiting Internet cafés for two or three hours after a 12-hour workday.

Contemporary article locator:
- https://news.sina.com.cn/c/sd/2010-10-11/180821252495.shtml

Evidence role:
- **B** for the report's account of the disclosed survey and participant observation;
- not an authenticated company timekeeping or payroll export.

This distinction is critical. A public portal article can preserve a worker's narrated actual-clock experience more strongly than a job advertisement, yet it still cannot answer whether every minute entered the employer's internal time record or payroll.

## 7. A contested 2008 case: schedule labels can hide employment-status differences

A 2008 China News report on a student investigation of Coca-Cola bottling operations described peak-season white/night shifts and claimed different crew/rest arrangements for formal and dispatched workers. The company disputed aspects of the investigation.

Locator:
- https://www.chinanews.com.cn/edu/xyztc/news/2008/12-26/1503603.shtml

Evidence role:
- **B, contested**.

Its methodological value is stronger than any single disputed number:

> **the same textual label (“两班”) can hide different rest topology for different employment categories.**

An archive schema should therefore never store only `shift_type=two_shift` and treat the problem as solved.

## 8. Policy pages: institutional possibility is not worker-level schedule evidence

The Ministry of Labour issued `劳部发〔1994〕503号`, establishing an approval framework for irregular and comprehensively calculated working-hour systems when enterprises cannot use standard working hours because of production characteristics.

Official current rehost of the normative text:
- https://www.mohrss.gov.cn/xxgk2020/fdzdgknr/zcfg/bmgz/202011/t20201102_394691.html

Evidence role:
- **A for the normative policy text**;
- the current webpage itself is not a 1994 Web artifact.

Hard boundary:

`policy permits/defines special working-hour system`  
≠ `enterprise received approval`  
≠ `job uses it`  
≠ `individual roster follows it`.

For 1995–2015 Web archaeology, later official rehosting may help recover the legal text, but it must not be described as an original 1994 website capture.

## 9. Old-Web communication can preserve schedule coordination — but timestamps are dangerous

Shift workers can use:

- BBS/forum posts;
- QQ/IM;
- SMS gateways;
- email;
- internal bulletin boards;
- later mobile messaging;

for shift swaps, rides, meals, social plans, complaints, job search or leisure.

But a common inference error is:

> **03:00 forum post ≠ author is a night-shift worker.**

A post timestamp alone cannot prove occupation, current location, wake state or reason for being online.

Similarly:

- “刚下班” in a forum post is a first-person claim, not a punch-clock record;
- a QQ status of “online” does not prove continuous active use;
- an Internet-café receipt would prove access at a time more directly, but not why the person was there;
- a job forum complaint about “我们两班倒” should not automatically be generalized to the whole employer without corroboration.

## 10. The Web may reduce schedule overlap requirements without restoring a shared clock

Asynchronous communication can matter especially for people who are free when others are not.

A forum post, email or offline QQ message can wait for the recipient. This lowers the requirement that two people be simultaneously free.

That is a plausible **usage-context mechanism**:

```text
shared-clock communication
→ message can be left asynchronously
→ some social contact survives low schedule overlap
```

But do not overclaim:

- public Web evidence rarely records whether a particular relationship was actually preserved by asynchronous messaging;
- later mobile/work messaging can also reach workers during daytime recovery sleep;
- network availability does not create physical co-presence for childcare, meals or family events.

For the ordinary-life synthesis, this mechanism belongs mainly in `how-people-lived`; this repository records the Web evidence boundaries.

## 12. Normalization trap

Modern recruitment sites frequently expose normalized tags such as:

- 两班倒;
- 三班倒;
- 长白班;
- 上四休二;
- 做六休一;
- 排班制;
- 弹性工作;
- 大小周.

If a historical 2007 page contains only prose, a modern researcher may be tempted to map it to a present-day tag.

That is allowed as **researcher normalization** only if stored separately:

```yaml
historical_wording: "..."
normalized_schedule_type: rotating_two_shift
normalization_actor: researcher
normalization_confidence: medium
```

Never render the normalized field as though the old platform itself had that checkbox.

## 13. Archive capture limits

A verified historical capture could potentially establish:

- that a page existed at a captured time;
- the schedule language visible in that representation;
- page structure and fields if resources survive;
- platform/category context;
- possibly browser/charset requirements if independently recoverable.

It still does not establish:

- vacancy liveness;
- successful application;
- actual worker assignment;
- actual clock-in/out;
- unrecorded overtime;
- schedule swaps;
- family consequences;
- sleep/recovery;
- whether a worker remained in the job.

This is analogous to other state-gap models in this repository: the public artifact preserves an offer/interface, while the completed lived transaction is often private.

- actual worker punch records;

The missing private state is itself historically meaningful.

## 15. Historical visibility: why shift workers can look flatter than they lived

A public archive may preserve:

> “招聘操作工，两班倒，包住。”

A later historian might therefore reduce the life to three fields:

`operator / two-shift / dormitory`.

But the actual month may have contained:

- one week on days;
- two weeks on nights;
- a temporary overtime peak;
- one shift swapped with a roommate;
- a missed family visit;
- a bus no longer running after clock-out;
- three daytime hospital visits after night work;
- Internet-café time after a shift;
- an unpaid extra hour;
- a sudden resignation.

Those are the events that make the schedule a life rather than a label, and most are precisely the states least likely to survive on the public Web.

This produces a new archive-bias warning:

> **schedule-label survival can create false temporal regularity.**

The cleaner the surviving job ad looks, the easier it is to imagine the worker's month was equally regular.

## 17. Next research targets

Highest-value next evidence is not another generic “night shift is hard” article.

Prefer:

1. a verified historical 2005–2012 job listing that explicitly preserves schedule fields and platform metadata;
2. an employer handbook or public notice with rotation cycle and crew count;
3. an anonymized roster plus actual clock record for the same period;
4. a contemporaneous BBS/QQ/forum source where shift swapping can be independently contextualized without exposing private identities;
5. a historical Internet-café / transport / canteen schedule showing whether night workers had usable infrastructure after clock-out;
6. a platform revision where a schedule category/tag changed over time.

## Core rule

> **The Web can preserve the promise or label of a shift. It usually cannot preserve the worker's actual month.**

A rigorous reconstruction must therefore keep `advertised schedule`, `roster`, `actual clock`, `recognized overtime`, `paid time`, and `lived recovery` as separate states.
